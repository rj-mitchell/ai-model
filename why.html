Capability compounds. Value doesn't.

Why the model never goes exponential, even when AI improves itself, and what the case for an economic singularity leaves out.

What these numbers measure

The model's headline figure is a multiple of what output would be without further AI, meaning a world where AI stays at its end-2026 level and level of use. "– by 2050" means the economy produces that many times more than it would have without further AI.

The GDP lines add growth from everything except AI to both worlds: – a year, the current average for advanced economies in the IMF's October 2026 outlook. That keeps the comparison like with like: GDP with AI against GDP without further AI.

The clearest way to see the result is as a growth rate. A singularity means the growth rate keeps rising. In the model, AI adds a lot to growth at first and then less and less.

Adoption spreads slowly at first, and early spending on training, integration and process change holds measured output back: the productivity J-curve. AI is a large, one-off boost spread over a few decades, closer to electrification than to a takeoff.

The explosion story in one paragraph

The singularity argument runs like this. AI gets better at doing AI research. Better AI makes the next AI arrive sooner. Each turn of the loop is faster than the last, so capability goes vertical, and because AI can do economic work, the growth rate of output keeps rising with it.

Every step in that chain is a multiplication: more capability, times more research, times more output. The model treats the economy the same way, as a chain of multiplications, and a chain like that moves only as fast as its slowest link. The singularity story assumes every link speeds up together. The model asks what happens to the links that don't.

An economy is not a single task. It is millions of tasks that depend on each other, and the slowest ones set the pace.

Seven things the explosion story misses

1

Capability is not value

The model separates T, how much useful work AI can do per pound, from V, how much more real output the economy produces. Singularity arguments usually treat these as the same number. In the default run, T grows – by 2041 and – by 2050, while output reaches – and then – what it would be without further AI.

Turning capability into output needs everything else in the economy to change with it: the work that AI can't do, the people who check AI's work, the infrastructure it runs on, and the organisations that have to rebuild around it.

2

The work AI can't touch sets the ceiling

This is Amdahl's law, first stated for parallel computers. If a share of a job can't be sped up, the whole job can never go faster than one divided by that share, however fast the rest becomes. If 20% of the work is irreducible, the most you can ever get is 5×.

In the model every sector keeps some irreducible share, even after AI has broadened as far as it can and workflows are fully redesigned. Physical work, care, regulation and judgement don't vanish. The table shows each of the six sectors' limits with infinitely capable AI. The ceilings rest on AI exposure studies and estimates of what robots can do, so the exact figures are uncertain; the pattern, that the physical and essential sectors have the lowest ceilings, is the point.

3

What gets cheap shrinks

This is Baumol's cost disease. When AI makes an essential service such as trade and logistics far more productive, its price falls, and spending shifts toward the essentials that stayed expensive, because people need all of them and can't swap one for another. In the model Trade & logistics' share of spending falls from – in 2027 to – by 2041, while Health, education & government rises from – to –. The slow essentials end up with more of the economy, so their slow growth weighs on the economy's growth.

The flexible sectors are treated as substitutes, so there the fastest sector wins: Knowledge & professional services grows from – to – of spending by 2050, mostly at the expense of property and hospitality. But it never comes to dominate, because the slow essential core still takes the biggest share of spending.

Historically this is why manufacturing productivity gains did not make whole economies explode: manufacturing's share of spending fell while services, which improved slowly, took over.

4

Essential sectors are weak links

Some sectors can't be substituted: power, payments, healthcare, logistics. If one of them fails, everything built on top of it suffers. In the model's sustained cyber-attack scenario, which pushes Finance & business services' exposure beyond anything measured, flexible sectors still reach – by 2041. But the essential core manages only –, and the economy – against – without the attack. Finance & business services is only a fifth of the core, yet the damage spreads across it.

Weak essential infrastructure also slows AI itself, because data centres need power, chips and payments. In that scenario useful AI work reaches – by 2041, against – in the default run. Explosive growth needs the slowest essential sector to keep pace with the fastest, and essential sectors are usually physical, regulated and slow.

5

Some output is an arms race, and output needs checking

Part of the economy is zero-sum. Better AI in advertising, litigation or cyber-attack mostly makes the other side spend more too. In those sectors extra capability raises costs without creating wealth. At 100× today's capability, Finance & business services' work gets – faster but its value rises only –, because trading, fraud and competition absorb much of the gain. Physical production gets only – faster but keeps far more of it: – more value. In the model, legal and advertising work sit inside Knowledge & professional services and pull its real-wealth share down.

Output also has to be verified. Cheap generation creates a review burden that lands on people, and some of it lands on whoever receives the spam, fraud or synthetic content. Measuring AI in tokens hides this: ten times more tokens with subtle errors can be worth less than before.

6

Self-improvement runs into the same walls

The recursive loop is real in the model: the AI R&D sector's productivity feeds next year's AI progress. But AI research is itself a sector with an irreducible share: compute, experiments, data, energy and human judgement about what to try. Its value tops out, so the loop strengthens AI progress without making it explode.

Turning the feedback from off to its maximum takes useful AI work in 2041 from – to –. The economy moves from – to –. Self-improvement changes how capable AI gets far more than it changes what the economy can absorb. Research productivity has also been falling for decades: each new idea takes more researchers than the last, which works against any loop.

7

Adoption, redesign and building take time

About – of the economy's tasks were done with AI at the end of 2026 in the model. Use spreads along an S-curve, slowly at first, and adopting and redesigning cost money and time before they pay off, so the gains arrive years after the capability does. Physical sectors only start re-wiring once AI is many times better than today. Chips, data centres and power stations can only be built so fast, which caps how quickly AI capability can grow at all.

None of these lags stops growth. Together they spread it over decades, and in the meantime the ceilings above are waiting.

What you would have to believe for it to explode

The model can produce explosive growth. To get it you have to remove every bottleneck in every sector at once: no arms races, no slop, and an irreducible share of work close to zero everywhere, including energy, healthcare and law. The table shows the ceiling on output, as a multiple of no further AI, with infinitely capable AI as the irreducible share in every sector shrinks.

To reach a hundredfold economy, more than 99% of all work, physical and social as well as digital, has to become something AI does at the speed of software. That is the real claim behind an economic singularity. It is a claim about plumbing, surgery, permitting, trust and verification, not a claim about how intelligent AI becomes.

The singularity is not a prediction about intelligence. It is a prediction that nothing in the economy stays slow.

What the explosion side gets right

  • Growth has accelerated before: over the long run of history, growth rates rose as population and ideas fed each other.
  • Standard growth models do produce explosive growth if AI can do every task, including research and physical work. The model agrees: set every sector's irreducible share near zero and output takes off.
  • AI broadens what it can do. The model lets native fit rise with capability, and some of today's walls will fall.
  • Today's AI is already under-used. Catching up alone is worth about – in the model, even with no further progress.

What would change the conclusion

  • Productivity in physical and regulated sectors, such as construction, healthcare and energy, starting to accelerate rather than lag.
  • AI checking its own work reliably, so that review burden falls as output rises.
  • Research productivity rising for the first time in decades.
  • Chips, data centres and power being built faster than any infrastructure in history.
  • Robotics making the irreducible share of physical work shrink year after year.
Corollary

Here's why we (probably) don't all die

The conversation this model grew out of started with AI doom. The economic argument above has a corollary for it. The fastest catastrophe stories need the same thing as the singularity story: AI that turns raw capability into power over the physical world quickly. The bottlenecks that slow the boom also slow that.

1

Power runs through the slow sectors

Taking control of anything that matters means energy, chips, factories, robots, money and people's cooperation. Those are the essential sectors with the lowest ceilings and the slowest redesign. In the model, physical production and energy keeps – of its work out of AI's reach even with unlimited capability.

2

Capability arrives faster than control

Useful AI work grows about – by 2050 while output grows –. That gap is the distance between what AI can do and what it is actually trusted and wired up to do. It closes over decades, through adoption decisions made by people and institutions.

3

Slow change produces warning shots

When AI spreads gradually, its failures show up first at small scale, where people can see them and respond. That is already happening: courts have sanctioned lawyers in more than 2,300 cases of AI-invented citations, and clinicians correct errors in AI-drafted notes. Failures at small scale lead to rules, audits and better tools before the stakes rise.

4

Unreliability limits autonomy

The same verification problem that holds down AI's economic value limits how far it can act on its own. Only about 2% of AI uses in UK finance are fully autonomous. Long chains of unsupervised, high-stakes action are exactly where today's AI is least dependable.

5

Dependence cuts both ways

Weak links constrain AI too. Any AI system depends on power grids, chip supply and data centres that people build, run and can switch off. The model's weak-link structure that drags down the economy in a crisis also makes AI's own expansion hostage to infrastructure it does not control.

What this does not rule out

  • Catastrophe doesn't need a growth explosion. An engineered pandemic or an attack on a weak link needs one dangerous capability, not a transformed economy. The model's own cyber-attack scenario shows how damage to one essential sector spreads to everything built on it.
  • Capability still compounds. Useful AI work growing about – by 2050 is fast enough to outrun institutions that react slowly.
  • People are a risk too. Misuse and concentration of power by humans don't require autonomous AI at all.
  • The model has no term for misaligned goals. Its catastrophe setting is an overlay you choose, not a result.

Why "probably"

In a 2023 survey of about 2,700 AI researchers, the median answer put the chance of human extinction or a similarly bad outcome from advanced AI at around 5%, and between 38% and 51% of respondents gave at least 10%, depending on how the question was worded. That is not small. The bottlenecks make a sudden, total takeover less likely. They do not make AI safe. Mostly, they buy time, and what matters is how that time is used.

How to read this

The ceilings in the model are assumptions, set deliberately to show which limits capability alone does not remove. The numbers are scenarios from guessed inputs, not forecasts. The point is the shape: every route to explosive growth runs through the slowest parts of the economy, so that is where the evidence should be looked for.
