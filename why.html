Capability compounds. Value doesn't.

Why the model never goes exponential, even when AI improves itself, and what the case for an economic singularity leaves out.

What these numbers measure

The headline figure is GDP as a multiple of GDP in a world where AI stays at its end-2026 level and use. "– by 2050" means the economy produces that many times as much as it would without further AI. It includes the extra machines, buildings and data centres that higher output pays for, which add – by 2050.

GDP counts everything people pay for, including activity that is only an arms race, such as rival advertising or litigation. The model also reports useful output, which leaves that out: – by 2050.

Both GDP lines include – a year of growth from everything except AI, the current average for advanced economies (IMF, October 2026), so the comparison is like with like.

The clearest way to see the result is as a growth rate. A singularity means the growth rate keeps rising. In the model, AI adds a lot to growth at first and then less and less.

GDP growth heads back toward its trend. AI is a large, one-off boost spread over a few decades, closer to electrification than to a takeoff.

The explosion story in one paragraph

The singularity argument runs like this. AI gets better at doing AI research. Better AI makes the next AI arrive sooner. Each turn of the loop is faster than the last, so capability goes vertical, and because AI can do economic work, the growth rate of output keeps rising with it.

Every step in that chain is a multiplication: more capability, times more research, times more output. The model treats the economy the same way, as a chain of multiplications, and a chain like that moves only as fast as its slowest link. The singularity story assumes every link speeds up together. The model asks what happens to the links that don't.

An economy is not a single task. It is millions of tasks that depend on each other, and the slowest ones set the pace.

Seven things the explosion story misses

1

Capability is not value

The model separates T, how much useful work AI can do per dollar, from V, how much more the economy produces. Singularity arguments usually treat these as the same number. In the default run, T grows – by 2041 and – by 2050, while GDP reaches – and then – what it would be without further AI.

Turning capability into output needs everything else in the economy to change with it: the work that AI can't do, the people who check AI's work, the infrastructure it runs on, and the organisations that have to rebuild around it.

2

The work AI can't touch sets the ceiling

If part of a job can't be sped up, the whole job can never go faster than one divided by that part, however fast the rest becomes. If 20% of the work stays as slow as ever, the most you can ever get is 5×.

In the model every sector keeps some irreducible share, even after AI has broadened as far as it can and everything that can be rebuilt around it has been. Physical work, care, regulation and judgement don't vanish. The table shows each of the six sectors' limits with infinitely capable AI. The ceilings rest on AI exposure studies and estimates of what robots can do, so the exact figures are uncertain; the pattern, that the physical and essential sectors have the lowest ceilings, is the point.

3

What gets cheap doesn't grow

When AI makes something far more productive, its price falls, and people don't buy proportionally more of it. Knowledge & professional services becomes about – as productive per worker by 2050, yet its share of spending rises only from – to –: its prices fall almost as fast as its output rises. The slow essential sectors, which people need whatever happens, keep most of the spending, so their slow growth weighs on the economy's growth.

Workers follow the same logic. When one sector needs fewer people, they move to the sectors that still need them, which are the slow ones. The model includes this, and it barely changes the result, because moving people into slow work doesn't make that work faster.

Historically this is why manufacturing productivity gains did not make whole economies explode: manufacturing's share of spending fell while services, which improved slowly, took over.

4

The weakest essential sector holds the rest back

Some sectors can't be substituted: power, payments, healthcare, logistics. If one of them fails, everything built on top of it suffers. In the model's sustained cyber-attack scenario, which pushes Finance & business services' exposure beyond anything measured, attacks take – of that sector's output by 2041. Flexible sectors still reach –, but the essential sectors manage only –. Finance & business services is only a fifth of the essential sectors, yet the damage spreads across them.

Weak essential infrastructure can also slow AI itself, because data centres need power, chips and payments: in the model, AI progress slows whenever essential sectors fall below where they would be without further AI. Explosive growth needs the slowest essential sector to keep pace with the fastest, and essential sectors are usually physical, regulated and slow.

5

Some output is an arms race, and output needs checking

Part of the economy is zero-sum. Better AI in advertising, litigation or cyber-attack mostly makes the other side spend more too. In those sectors extra capability raises costs without creating wealth. By 2050, Finance & business services' work gets – faster and GDP records – more output, but its useful output rises only –, because trading, fraud and competition absorb much of the gain. Physical production gets only – faster but keeps nearly all of it: –. In the model, legal and advertising work sit inside Knowledge & professional services and pull down the share of its work that creates real wealth.

Output also has to be verified. Cheap generation creates a review burden that lands on people, and some of it lands on whoever receives the spam, fraud or synthetic content. Checking doesn't get faster as AI gets cheaper, so it becomes a bottleneck of its own: in the model, AI work that needs expert review can never get more than about – faster, however cheap the AI, because checking it still takes people's time.

6

Self-improvement runs into the same walls

The loop is real in the model: the AI research sector's productivity feeds next year's AI progress. But AI research is itself a sector with an irreducible share: compute, experiments, data, energy and human judgement about what to try. Its value tops out, so the loop strengthens AI progress without making it explode.

Turning that feedback from off to its strongest takes useful AI work in 2050 from – to –. GDP moves only from – to –. Self-improvement changes how capable AI gets far more than it changes what the economy can absorb. Research productivity has also been falling for decades: each new idea takes more researchers than the last, which works against any loop.

7

Adoption and rebuilding take time

About – of the economy's tasks were done with AI at the end of 2026 in the model. Use spreads along an S-curve, slowly at first, and adopting and rebuilding cost money and time before they pay off, so the gains arrive years after the capability does. Physical sectors only start rebuilding once AI is many times better than today. Chips, data centres and power stations can only be built so fast, which caps how quickly AI capability can grow at all.

Adoption speeds up as AI gets better, because a bigger payoff makes organisations move faster, but it still takes years. It also comes in two stages. First, AI speeds up tasks inside existing processes, where people still direct and check the work, so with today's AI those tasks get at most about – faster. That limit loosens as AI becomes able to direct and check more of its own work. The bigger gains need the work itself rebuilt around AI: new plant and equipment, re-laid-out sites, replaced systems, rewritten rules and contracts, new roles. That is slow and sticky. Machines and buildings last decades, sites can't move, regulation and contracts have to be renegotiated, and people keep doing what works.

None of these lags stops growth. Together they spread it over decades, and in the meantime the ceilings above are waiting.

What about robots?

Most of the work AI can't reach is physical: building, making, moving, cooking, cleaning, caring. So robotics is the biggest question for this whole argument, and the model only partly includes it.

What is included. The ceilings on how much hands-on work AI could ever do already allow for some robotics: they combine AI exposure studies with McKinsey's 2025 estimate that robots using demonstrated technology could take on about 13% of US work hours. As AI improves, the model lets it reach more of each ceiling.

What is not. Robots are not modelled as a technology of their own, with their own cost curve, factories and installation time, and the ceilings are fixed. That amounts to assuming robots never take over most physical work. If that assumption is wrong, it is the most likely way the conclusion breaks.

To see how much rides on it, the "Robotics breakthrough" scenario lets robots take over most hands-on work in physical production, trade and logistics, health, education and government, and property and hospitality, as much as AI can do in information work.

Robots shift the picture more than anything else in the model. The share of physical production that stays out of reach falls from – to –, and the peak boost rises from – to – a year. Even then the economy does not explode: checking costs, arms races and the work that stays human still cap it at –.

Robots also run into the same bottlenecks as everything else, and in a stronger form. They have to be manufactured, shipped, installed, powered and maintained: physical production at physical speed, paid for with capital that competes with every other investment. Care, health and food safety need certification and public trust. Much of hospitality and personal services is millions of small firms that adopt slowly. So even a robotics breakthrough reaches the economy through its slowest sectors. The model's speed limit and adoption lags stand in for this build-out, but a robot-specific build-out would likely be slower.

Capable is not the same as used

Robots and AI could do much more work than they will actually be given, for three reasons.

  • Upfront cost. A machine has to beat a wage, not just match a skill. An MIT study found that about 80% of tasks involving vision are technically automatable, but at today's costs only 23% of the wages paid for them would be worth automating, and even with costs falling 20% a year most would take decades to pay off (MIT CSAIL, 2024).
  • People like dealing with people. Around 80% of Americans say they prefer a human to AI in customer service (SurveyMonkey, 2025), and the preference is strongest in care, health and anything personal. Much of hospitality and personal service is valued partly because a person provides it.
  • People like their jobs. Workers and voters resist automation that threatens them. After a strike in 2024, US East Coast dockworkers won a contract that keeps fully automated equipment out of their ports (Maritime Executive, 2025).

These frictions slow automation more than they stop it. Preferences soften as machines improve: most people who prefer a human agent say they would switch if automated service fully solved their problem (Metrigy, 2025). When the cost gap is wide enough, machines win: farming fell from about 41% of the US workforce in 1900 to under 2% by 2000. But they mean the model's ceilings, which describe what is technically possible, overstate what will actually be automated, especially in care, hospitality and personal services. Some of the work that stays human will stay human by choice, which adds to the irreducible share rather than shrinking it. The robotics breakthrough scenario assumes all of that resistance eventually gives way.

If robots start taking over a growing share of physical work year after year, the case against explosive growth gets much weaker. That is the evidence to watch.

When does the boom peak, compared with AGI?

The model's capability path follows the median expert. In the Forecasting Research Institute's 2026 panel of 339 AI experts, the median expert expects AI that beats a 90th-percentile professional on 90% of non-physical tasks around 2050, with the middle half of forecasts between 2039 and 2065, and reliable 8-hour software tasks around 2030. Forecasting sites and AI lab leaders expect AGI much sooner, so the table compares their timelines too.

Two things stand out. On the median expert's path, the boom peaks – years before AGI: most of the gains come from AI doing part of the work across many sectors, long before it can do nearly everything. Much earlier AGI matters a great deal for how big and how soon the boom is. On lab leaders' timelines, growth from AI peaks at – a year, against – on the median expert's path, and GDP is – what it would be without further AI by 2035, against –. More capable AI needs less direction from people, and makes adopting and rebuilding pay sooner. But adoption and rebuilding still take years, so the peak comes about – years after AGI, and in the first few years after AGI the economy has barely changed: the AI is capable, but not yet cheap or widely used. And every timeline is heading for the same ceiling, –, set by physical work, checking and arms races, which AGI for non-physical work does not lift.

That matches history. Factories took about 40 years to reorganise around electric motors, and computers were famously everywhere but in the productivity statistics until the late 1990s. A technology's biggest growth effect comes when organisations finish rebuilding around it, not when it is invented.

What you would have to believe for it to explode

The model can produce explosive growth. To get it you have to remove every bottleneck in every sector at once: no arms races, no checking costs, and an irreducible share of work close to zero everywhere, including energy, healthcare and law. The table shows the ceiling on GDP, as a multiple of no further AI, with unlimited AI and with workers and capital fully adjusted, as the irreducible share in every sector shrinks.

To reach a hundredfold economy, about – of all work, physical and social as well as digital, has to become something AI does at the speed of software. That is the real claim behind an economic singularity. It is a claim about plumbing, surgery, permitting, trust and verification, not a claim about how intelligent AI becomes.

The singularity is not a prediction about intelligence. It is a prediction that nothing in the economy stays slow.

Which limits matter

A fair criticism of any model like this is that it builds in the answer. So here is every limit in the model, removed one at a time, then all together. One limit dominates: how much of each sector's work AI can ever do. Lift it so AI can eventually do 95% of every sector's work and 2050 GDP rises to –, with a ceiling of –. Everything else moves the 2050 figure by a tenth or two. Remove every limit at once and the model does take off: – by 2050, growing – a year at the peak, with a ceiling of –. The model can explode. It just needs nearly every kind of work, physical and social as well as digital, to become something AI can do.

Objections, and how the model answers them

A critique of an earlier version of this model raised the points below. Several were right, and the model has changed because of them.

  1. "It hard-codes the plateau." Partly true, and deliberate: the ceilings on what AI can do are fixed, because the point is to show which limits capability alone doesn't remove. The table above shows exactly what each one costs. Only the ceiling on AI-ready work matters much.
  2. "Essential sectors are far too complementary." The model treats them as near-perfect complements. Studies of broad sectors and of industries' inputs to each other find very low substitutability, so this is in line with the evidence, and it barely matters: letting sectors substitute freely gives – in 2050 against –, because AI raises every sector's productivity by broadly similar amounts.
  3. "Freed-up workers would move into the bottleneck sectors." Right, and now modelled: workers drift toward the sectors that need them, closing about a fifth of the gap a year. It changes little (– without it), because moving people into slow work doesn't speed that work up. That is the cost disease, not a cure for it.
  4. "There is no capital accumulation." Right, and the most important correction. Higher output now pays for more machines, buildings and data centres. That raises 2050 GDP from – to – and the long-run ceiling from – to –. It amplifies the boost without making it explode, because capital can't make the slow work fast either.
  5. "Arms races count in GDP." Right. The model now reports both: GDP, which counts rival advertising and litigation as output, and useful output, which doesn't.
  6. "Diminishing returns rule out self-improvement by assumption." They are now set from evidence that research gets harder over time, and AI research feeds back into progress. Removing them entirely makes AI far more capable but gives only – by 2050: the economy can't absorb capability faster than it can rebuild around it.
  7. "Checking and attack costs grew with your own speed, forcing a peak." Right, and fixed. Checking is now people's time spent verifying AI's work, and attacks take a bounded share of output that defensive AI partly offsets. Output now levels off as AI improves, instead of peaking and falling.
  8. "Defence never catches up with offence." Fixed: defensive AI now removes up to half of attack losses as AI broadens. Even perfect defence only lifts 2050 GDP to –.
  9. "One number did too much." Right. What AI can do (set by the AGI year) and how cheaply it does it (set by the speed of AI progress) are now separate.
  10. Two problems the critique missed. Rebuilding work around AI could push a sector past the ceiling on what AI can do, and essential sectors could slow AI progress but never speed it up. Both are fixed: rebuilding now applies only to work AI already does, and cheaper energy, chips and construction now speed AI's build-out.

What the explosion side gets right

  • Growth has accelerated before: over the long run of history, growth rates rose as population and ideas fed each other.
  • Standard growth models do produce explosive growth if AI can do every task, including research and physical work. The model agrees: set every sector's irreducible share near zero and output takes off.
  • AI broadens what it can do. The model lets the share of work AI can do rise with capability, and some of today's walls will fall.
  • Today's AI is already under-used. Catching up alone is worth about – in the model, even with no further progress.
  • Richer economies invest more. The extra capital that AI's output pays for adds – to GDP by 2050, and lifts the long-run ceiling from – to –.

What would change the conclusion

Each would have to last for years, not one good quarter. None is happening yet.

  • Construction, health care and energy getting faster every year. Output per worker rising 5% a year or more, five years running. Measured US construction productivity has been falling for decades (Goolsbee and Syverson, 2023).
  • AI trusted to act alone where mistakes are costly. Most AI use in finance, medicine and law running without human sign-off, accepted by regulators and insurers. In UK finance it is about 2% (Bank of England and FCA, 2024).
  • Discoveries arriving several times faster. New medicines, for example, approved at several times the recent US rate of about 50 a year, without more spent on research. So far each new idea has needed more researchers than the last (Bloom et al., 2020).
  • Power built at wartime speed. Electricity supply growing 10% a year or more for several years. US generation has grown less than 1% a year on average since 2007 (EIA).
  • Robots taking over hands-on work outside factories. Jobs in care, cleaning, construction and hospitality shrinking year after year while demand for them rises. Today about half a million industrial robots are installed a year worldwide, almost all in factories (IFR, 2024).
Corollary

Here's why we (probably) don't all die

Much of the worry about AI is about catastrophe, not growth. The economic argument above has a corollary for it. The fastest catastrophe stories need the same thing as the singularity story: AI that turns raw capability into power over the physical world quickly. The bottlenecks that slow the boom also slow that.

1

Power runs through the slow sectors

Taking control of anything that matters means energy, chips, factories, robots, money and people's cooperation. Those are the essential sectors with the lowest ceilings and the slowest rebuilding. In the model, physical production and energy keeps – of its work out of AI's reach even with unlimited capability.

2

Capability arrives faster than control

Useful AI work grows about – by 2050 while GDP grows –. That gap is the distance between what AI can do and what it is actually trusted and wired up to do. It closes over decades, through adoption decisions made by people and institutions.

3

Slow change produces warning shots

When AI spreads gradually, its failures show up first at small scale, where people can see them and respond. That is already happening: courts have sanctioned lawyers in more than 2,300 cases of AI-invented citations, and clinicians correct errors in AI-drafted notes. Failures at small scale lead to rules, audits and better tools before the stakes rise.

4

Unreliability limits autonomy

The same verification problem that holds down AI's economic value limits how far it can act on its own. Only about 2% of AI uses in UK finance are fully autonomous. Long chains of unsupervised, high-stakes action are exactly where today's AI is least dependable. That brake weakens as the unreliability is engineered out, which is good for usefulness, and means safety then has to come from AI reliably doing what people intend rather than from it being too clumsy to act alone.

5

Dependence cuts both ways

AI depends on essential infrastructure too: power grids, chip supply and data centres that people build, run and can switch off. The same dependence that drags down the economy in a crisis makes AI's own expansion hostage to infrastructure it does not control.

What this does not rule out

  • Catastrophe doesn't need a growth explosion. An engineered pandemic or an attack on essential infrastructure needs one dangerous capability, not a transformed economy. The model's own cyber-attack scenario shows how damage to one essential sector spreads to everything built on it.
  • Capability still compounds. Useful AI work growing about – by 2050 is fast enough to outrun institutions that react slowly.
  • People are a risk too. Misuse and concentration of power by humans don't require autonomous AI at all.
  • The model has no term for misaligned goals. It models output if things broadly go right, so it cannot put a number on catastrophic risk.

Why "probably"

In a 2023 survey of about 2,700 AI researchers, the median answer put the chance of human extinction or a similarly bad outcome from advanced AI at around 5%, and between 38% and 51% gave at least 10%, depending on how the question was worded. That is not small.

There is good news beyond the bottlenecks. Much of the risk from today's AI comes from it being unreliable: misreading what it was asked, cutting corners, making things up, being talked into things. Those failings shrink as models get more capable and as training aimed at them improves, so smarter models are generally better at doing what people intend.

The open question is whether that holds once AI is capable enough to act on its own for long stretches. Some tests find more capable models better at gaming their evaluations or hiding what they are doing (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Meinke et al., 2024). The bottlenecks don't settle that. They buy time for alignment research and for rules to catch up, and make it more likely that problems show up while they are still small.

How to read this

The ceilings in the model are assumptions, set deliberately to show which limits capability alone does not remove. The numbers are scenarios from uncertain inputs, not forecasts. The point is the shape: every route to explosive growth runs through the slowest parts of the economy, so that is where the evidence should be looked for.

Every number on this page is calculated from the model's default settings. Open the model to change them, or get the results as data: data.json, data.csv, model.json and a plain-text summary, llms.txt.
